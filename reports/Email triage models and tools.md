# Rules first, small classifiers second, Qwen last

The best way to triage a polytechnic student's inbox privately is not a single model but a **cascade**. Header and sender rules come first. A small local classifier trained on the student's own labelled mail comes second. Qwen3 8B on llama.cpp comes last, used mainly to *extract* actions and deadlines into a constrained JSON schema, with dates resolved by deterministic code rather than the model. No off-the-shelf model or public dataset matches the target scheme of 8 categories (action_required, deadline, notice, opportunity, personal, system, newsletter, other) and 4 priorities (urgent, soon, whenever, ignore). The open email classifiers that exist are hobby projects trained on about 2,000 emails, and the new September 2026 "decision models" (Laya, GLiNER2.5-Decide) score only 47–60% exact-match zero-shot on mixed domains. That makes **labelling 300–1,000 of the student's own emails** the step that matters most. Throughput is not a constraint at about 150 emails a month, since even CPU inference at 200–500 ms per email is negligible. The real constraints are label quality, calibration, and avoiding duplicate deadlines across email and Brightspace. For the email side, borrow designs rather than code from existing projects: Inbox Zero's hybrid rules, ollamail's category + priority + reason output, and AgCalender's human-approval loop. On the LMS side, RohanMuppa's MIT-licensed brightspace-mcp-server, which already has a Ngee Ann Polytechnic sign-in preset, is the one dependency worth taking.

## No ready-made classifier fits an 8+4 student taxonomy

The open-weight email classifiers on Hugging Face are few and small. The most useful is **Ippoboi/mmbert-base-email-classifier**. It is an ONNX, CPU-friendly mmBERT model with two heads: six Gmail-style categories (PERSONAL, NEWSLETTER, PROMOTIONAL, TRANSACTION, ALERT, SOCIAL) and a sigmoid **"action required" probability with a tuned threshold of 0.525**. Its v9 release feeds the sender to the model, keeps link domains, strips boilerplate footers and truncates to a token budget ([HF](https://huggingface.co/Ippoboi/mmbert-base-email-classifier)). Each of those preprocessing choices is worth copying. A sibling Flan-T5-small model by the same author was trained on just **2,043 hand-labelled personal emails** and reports no accuracy figure ([README](https://huggingface.co/Ippoboi/gmail-classifier/blob/main/README.md)). The 10-class models from imnim (a LoRA adapter on Llama-3.1-8B) and byinab (DistilBERT) were both trained on a **synthetic set of 2,105 emails** and publish no metrics ([imnim](https://huggingface.co/imnim/multi-label-email-classifier); [byinab](https://huggingface.co/byinab/custom-email-classifier/blob/main/config.json)). Mapped onto the target scheme, these models cover newsletter, personal and system reasonably well, and Ippoboi's action head approximates action_required. **Nothing covers deadline, notice, opportunity, or any priority level.**

The general zero-shot route is real but weak on a custom scheme. MoritzLaurer's DeBERTa-v3 and ModernBERT zeroshot-v2.0 NLI models run on CPU and take arbitrary labels ([HF collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/MoritzLaurer/zeroshot-classifiers)). They need descriptive hypotheses such as "This email asks me to do something" rather than a bare label like action_required. They also cannot reason about priority, because priority depends on dates and on who sent the mail. The September 2026 wave of typed-question decision models fits the schema shape well, since one `choice` question can cover the 8 categories and a second can cover the 4 priorities in a single pass. **Laya** (Apache-2.0; ModernBERT-large 421M, or an mmBERT-base 322M multilingual variant) reaches 0.993 accuracy on spam but only **0.522 on 10-way support-ticket routing**, and runs at **193–464 ms per request on CPU** ([mer.vin](https://mer.vin/news/laya-the-33ms-open-source-decision-model-beating-jev/)). **GLiNER2.5-Decide** (340M, Apache-2.0, English only) leads a 17-domain exact-match benchmark at **60.2%**. That puts it ahead of a Qwen3.5-4B baseline at 56.4% and Laya Router at 46.6%, but none of the 17 domains is identified as email ([soniqo.audio](https://soniqo.audio/blog/gliner-decide-vs-jev)). These models are a few weeks old and their numbers are vendor- or blog-reported. Jev, the closed model that prompted them, is hosted-API only and so does not fit a privacy-first design ([aimlapi](https://aimlapi.com/blog/what-is-jev)).

The limited head-to-head evidence favours small fine-tuned encoders. A corporate email-intent study reported a fine-tuned XLM-RoBERTa at **F1 0.78 against 0.73 for an LLM pipeline**, with roughly half the false positives and no per-call cost ([INJOIT](https://injoit.ru/index.php/j1/article/view/2576)). That figure comes from a search summary and the paper itself was not read, so treat it as directional. Fine-tuning also lifts 7–8B LLMs sharply: one study reports Qwen-7B gaining 8.5–38.6 accuracy points across classification datasets ([GNU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gnu.ac.kr/handle/sw.gnu/79411)). Among open 7–8B models, Proofpoint's EmailBench found **Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct beat Llama-3.1-8B** on email understanding ([Proofpoint](https://www.proofpoint.com/us/blog/engineering-insights/introducing-emailbench-open-source-benchmark-email-understanding)). That supports the existing choice of the Qwen family, though no Qwen3-8B email numbers were found.

## Choosing the classifier route: a staged decision

The three candidate routes trade off differently. The research turned up no email benchmark for embedding + logistic regression, so its row below is reasoned from first principles rather than measured.

| Route | Strengths | Weaknesses | Role in this app |
|---|---|---|---|
| Header/sender rules | Free, near-perfect for bulk mail and LMS notifications | No semantics; misses deadlines buried in newsletters | Tier 0 for system/newsletter |
| Embedding + logistic regression (e.g. a local embedding model via llama.cpp) | Trains in seconds on a few hundred labels; probabilities are easy to calibrate; retrain after every correction | Collapses an email to one vector, which is weak for "is there a deadline in paragraph 4"; no benchmark found | Tier 1 category classifier once ~300 labels exist |
| Fine-tuned small encoder (mmBERT/ModernBERT/DeBERTa, two heads) | Best evidence on email intent; CPU-viable; Ippoboi shows the dual-head pattern | Needs a GPU session or patience to fine-tune; needs ~1–2k labels to be clearly better | Upgrade path after a semester of labels |
| Zero-shot decision model (Laya, GLiNER2.5-Decide) | No training; typed questions match the schema; Laya ships temperature fitting | 47–60% mixed-domain zero-shot; immature; must be temperature-scaled | Pre-labeller to speed up annotation |
| Qwen3 8B constrained JSON + logprobs | Handles the reasoning-heavy labels (opportunity, deadline) and extraction in one call | Slowest; grammar output carries no calibrated confidence unless logprobs are read | Tier 2 for residual mail and all extraction |

The recommended path follows from this table. **Start with rules plus Qwen3 8B** so the app is useful on day one. Use Qwen's outputs, or a decision model's, as **pre-labels the student corrects**. Once a few hundred corrected labels exist, train an **embedding + logistic regression** category model. It is the cheapest learner that improves with every correction, which matters because domain shift is the main risk: Microsoft's commitment-detection work found Enron-trained models degrade on Avocado mail ([MSR](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/email-overload-using-machine-learning-to-manage-messages-commitments/)). Graduate to a **fine-tuned two-head encoder** only if the learning curve plateaus. Keep priority out of the classifier entirely. Compute it from extracted due dates relative to today, the sender's standing (lecturer, LMS, unknown), and the category, because a classifier cannot know what "Friday" means.

### Rules settle most bulk mail before any model runs

Mail providers converge on the same signals: who sent it, what type of content it is, and how the user has engaged with that sender ([Google Workspace blog](https://workspace.google.com/blog/productivity-collaboration/how-gmail-sorts-your-email-based-on-your-preferences)). The cheapest local equivalents are RFC headers. Since the 2024 Gmail/Yahoo bulk-sender rules, large senders must support RFC 8058 one-click unsubscribe, so `List-Unsubscribe` and `List-Unsubscribe-Post` reliably flag newsletters ([Mailgun](https://www.mailgun.com/blog/deliverability/what-is-rfc-8058/)). Per RFC 3834, `Precedence: bulk|list|junk` and `Auto-Submitted` flag automated mail ([IANA](https://www.iana.org/assignments/message-headers/perm-headers.html)). Senders' schema.org markup (EventReservation, Invoice, ParcelDelivery) carries **exact structured dates** when present ([Google Developers](https://developers.google.com/gmail/markup/highlights)). Two further signals separate personal mail from bulk: whether the student has ever replied to the sender, which is the core of HEY's Screener and Outlook's Focused Inbox, and To-vs-CC placement ([HEY](https://www.hey.com/how-it-works/)). Brightspace's instructor-configured **Intelligent Agents** send deadline-reminder emails ([MUN CITL](https://blog.citl.mun.ca/technologyresources/tech-res-dev/intelligent-agents-introduction/)), so POLITEMall sender patterns deserve their own rule that links the email to an LMS item rather than creating a new one.

### Constrain Qwen's output, then read its logprobs for confidence

llama-server converts a subset of JSON Schema to a GBNF grammar through `response_format` on `/chat/completions`. Two caveats from the docs matter here. **The schema is not injected into the prompt**, so the prompt must still describe every field. And nested `$ref` and `prefixItems` are broken ([llama.cpp grammars README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/grammars/README.md)). In practice, use a flat object with `category` and `priority` as enums, short arrays with `maxItems`, and an optional short `reason` placed before the label fields, which gives the model a little room to reason inside the constraint. Grammar decoding masks invalid tokens ([ZeroEntropy](https://www.zeroentropy.dev/concepts/constrained-decoding/)), so the chosen enum value says nothing about confidence. **LLM logprob classification** fixes that. Have the category be the first token-distinguishable field, request token probabilities from llama-server, and renormalise over the 8 enum values. The result is a probability vector that can be calibrated like any classifier's. If the label strings share leading tokens, map them to single-token codes. Self-consistency over 2–3 samples is a costlier fallback. Have Qwen extract the **verbatim date span** ("by EOD next Fri") rather than an ISO date, and resolve it with `dateparser`, using `RELATIVE_BASE` set to the email's Date header and `PREFER_DATES_FROM='future'` ([dateparser docs](https://dateparser.readthedocs.io/en/stable/settings.html)). Small models are unreliable at calendar arithmetic. Every mature student-deadline project in the notes moves date math into code for exactly this reason: AgCalender's "All date math lives here", and Adamolids computes `daysUntilDue` in code ([AgCalender](https://github.com/shabajsha/AgCalender); [Adamolids](https://github.com/Adamolids/Brightspace_Assignment_Tracker)).

## Calibration and labelling decide whether thresholds can be trusted

Calibration is never automatic, even for models marketed as calibrated. Laya "ships over-confident". Temperature fitting cut the base model's **expected calibration error from 0.466 to 0.081** ([mer.vin](https://mer.vin/news/laya-the-33ms-open-source-decision-model-beating-jev/)). Ippoboi's 0.525 action threshold likewise implies tuning on validation data. The practical recipe is to hold out about 20% of the student's labelled mail and fit a single temperature for each probabilistic source (logistic regression, encoder, Qwen logprobs). For each class, pick the threshold that hits a target precision on action_required and deadline. Recall matters most there, because a missed deadline costs far more than a false alarm. Then route anything below threshold, or anything where the rules and the model disagree, to a **needs_review** bucket rather than forcing a label. An n8n/llama3.1 triage project uses exactly this confidence-threshold fallback over 8 categories ([triagem-email-llm](https://github.com/ClaudineiAlves/triagem-email-llm)). Hosted Jev was right 94–96% of the time at confidence ≥0.9 ([aimlapi](https://aimlapi.com/blog/what-is-jev)). That is the kind of reliability curve to measure locally before auto-filing anything. With only ~150 emails a month, calibration estimates will be noisy for a full semester. Use few bins, and refit monthly.

Labelling has to be designed for consistency, not just volume. On Enron, annotators agreed on requests for action at **κ = 0.78 but on commitments only at κ = 0.54** ([Macquarie](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/can-requests-for-action-and-commitments-to-act-be-reliably-identi/)). Fuzzy notions like "opportunity" or "soon" will be harder still. Write a one-paragraph definition with a counterexample for each label, and let priority be partly computed rather than annotated. Weak supervision from the student's own actions is free labelling data. Microsoft's SIGIR 2020 work used interaction logs (for example, scheduling a meeting after a request) as noisy labels alongside clean ones ([arXiv 2005.13084](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.13084)). For this app, the equivalents are approving or skipping a proposed task, marking a newsletter "always ignore", or opening an opportunity link. A year of mail is roughly 1,800 emails, the same order as every existing hobby training set. Stratify the evaluation set (~200–500 emails) across all 8 categories, and measure the predictable hard pairs: **newsletter↔opportunity** (scholarships and events arrive inside digests) and **notice↔action_required**.

Public datasets can be used only for warm-starting. Enron (~500k emails) and the LDC-licensed Avocado corpus carry sentence-level request/commitment labels, not inbox categories, and their early-2000s corporate mail contains no LMS notifications ([arXiv 2210.16841](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.16841); [LDC](https://catalog.ldc.upenn.edu/LDC2015T03)). The Hugging Face priority sets, such as infinite-dataset-hub/EmailPriorityDetection (High/Medium/Low), are LLM-synthetic and have no "ignore" class ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/infinite-dataset-hub/EmailPriorityDetection)). Microsoft's lineage of action-item detection (2005), commitment detection (2019) and Smart To-Do (ACL 2020, ROUGE 0.63) provides the **extraction schema** more than the data ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.767)). That schema is `requests[]` (asks of the student, with due dates), `commitments[]` (promises the student made in sent mail), `events[]` and `deadlines[]` (hard or soft).

### Taxonomies: copy the orthogonality, invent "opportunity"

Gmail (Primary/Social/Promotions/Updates/Forums), Apple Mail (Primary/Transactions/Updates/Promotions), HEY (Imbox/Feed/Paper Trail) and SaneBox all split mail the same way: human, transactional, bulk ([Swarthmore KB](https://kb.swarthmore.edu/wiki/Gmail_Categories_and_Inbox_Tabs); [HEY](https://www.hey.com/how-it-works/)). Apple's design holds the key lesson. Transactional or promotional messages with **time-sensitive content also appear in Primary** ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/icloud-ipad/mmafbebd3108/icloud)), which is exactly why the target scheme should keep priority orthogonal to category. A newsletter announcing a scholarship that closes Friday is category newsletter or opportunity, but priority soon. No provider has an "opportunity" class, so that label needs its own few-shot examples and the most labelling effort. The deadline and action_required categories also overlap. Consider collapsing them into one category, action_required, and recording deadlines as extracted fields, which removes a hard confusion pair.

## Borrow designs from email assistants, take one LMS dependency

Among email assistants, only **Inbox Zero** is large and active (12.4k stars, pushed 2026-10-08). Its licence is AGPL-3.0 with extra commercial terms, and it supports Gmail and Outlook only, with no IMAP or .eml input ([GitHub](https://github.com/elie222/inbox-zero); [LICENSE](https://github.com/elie222/inbox-zero/blob/main/LICENSE)). Three of its ideas are worth taking. Its rules combine plain-language **AI conditions** with **static from/subject conditions**. A History tab "Fix" action rewrites a rule after a misfire ([docs](https://docs.getinboxzero.com/essentials/email-ai-personal-assistant)). And it uses tiered model slots (NANO/ECONOMY/DEFAULT/DRAFT) plus an `OPENAI_COMPATIBLE_BASE_URL` setting that points straight at llama-server ([.env.example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/elie222/inbox-zero/main/apps/web/.env.example)). Those tiers map cleanly to "Qwen3 8B for classification, cheap cloud for drafting". **ollamail** is the closest functional match. It outputs a category and a priority per mail, each with a stated reason, exports extracted tasks to CalDAV, and keeps mail content out of its logs. It is AGPL and tied to Postgres/pgvector, so treat it as a design reference ([GitHub](https://github.com/dusseligerdussel/ollamail)). Local-Email-Triage-Agent (MIT) pairs a distilled small classifier with **deterministic priority signals** ([GitHub](https://github.com/claudiogiu/Local-Email-Triage-Agent)), validating the "priority is computed" stance above. redei-ma/email-triage benchmarks keyword rules against local and cloud LLMs and hybrids on a held-out set ([GitHub](https://github.com/redei-ma/email-triage)), which is the evaluation harness to replicate. No mature project reads .eml files from disk natively, so the student's existing capture layer is the right foundation, with components bolted on rather than a whole app adopted.

The student-specific projects contribute the safety design. **AgCalender** (college email to calendar, local gemma2:9b) uses a keyword pre-filter, imports `.ics` attachments without an LLM, fingerprints processed mail in SQLite, and sends **every proposed item to Telegram with Add/Skip buttons**, offering to block senders the user always skips ([GitHub](https://github.com/shabajsha/AgCalender)). That skip signal is free weak supervision. **vidya** (MIT) adds invariants: no calendar write without review, no deletion unless an item is absent on two successful reads on different days, 11:59 PM pinned to the course timezone, and provenance (source URL, verbatim text, confidence) on every event ([GitHub](https://github.com/vinilpolepalli/vidya)). **canvas-mcp** wraps LMS-authored text in provenance fences as "data-not-instructions" to defend against prompt injection ([GitHub](https://github.com/vishalsachdev/canvas-mcp)). Email bodies carry the same risk, so they need the same fence. No project found merges email-derived and LMS-derived deadlines. The app needs its own merge key, for example orgUnitId + dropbox folder or quiz id for LMS items, with email items linked by course code + title + resolved date.

On the Brightspace side, **RohanMuppa/brightspace-mcp-server** (MIT, ~56 stars, pushed 2026-10-08) is the one component worth depending on. It is read-only, ships a `--ngeeann` preset described as "Plain Microsoft Entra", handles MFA, and keeps credentials in the OS keychain ([sign-in docs](https://github.com/RohanMuppa/brightspace-mcp-server/blob/main/docs/sign-in.md)). Its `token-mint.ts` converts session cookies into a Valence Bearer token via `/d2l/lp/auth/oauth2/token` with a CSRF header ([source](https://github.com/RohanMuppa/brightspace-mcp-server/blob/main/src/auth/token-mint.ts)). Its `weekly_briefing` prompt leads with anything due within 48 hours ([source](https://github.com/RohanMuppa/brightspace-mcp-server/blob/main/src/prompts/weekly-briefing.ts)). POLITEShop documents POLITEMall's Valence routes (le 1.75, lp 1.46) and internal Siren APIs, but it **has no licence**, so use it as reference only ([GitHub](https://github.com/haziq21/politeshop)). Whether POLITEMall's tenant accepts the cookie-to-token mint has not been independently confirmed, so test that first.

### Cloud fallback stays narrow and redacted

Cheap cloud models are useful for the residue the local cascade cannot settle, and for drafting. Before anything leaves the machine, redact it with **Microsoft Presidio**. Replace names, emails, phone numbers and student IDs with consistent placeholders, keep the mapping locally, and add custom recognisers for institutional identifiers. Presidio's own FAQ states there is "no guarantee" it finds all sensitive information ([Presidio FAQ](https://microsoft.github.io/presidio/faq/)), so redaction reduces risk but is not anonymisation. The better privacy control is volume: send only trimmed bodies of unresolved mail, never headers or full threads. For preprocessing, use `inscriptis` (Apache-2.0) for HTML-to-text. It keeps the tables and lists where newsletter event listings live ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/inscriptis/)). Strip quoted replies with a small regex set, or with patterns vendored from Mailgun's `talon`, which appears inactive since 2017 ([PyPI](https://pypi.python.org/pypi/talon)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the question. The question asked which classifier to choose, but the evidence says the classifier is the least important component. Every hard part of student email triage is something no model provides. Priority depends on date arithmetic, so it belongs in code. "Opportunity" and the student's sense of urgency exist in no public taxonomy or dataset, so they must come from the student's own corrections. Deadline duplication across email and Brightspace is a data-modelling problem. The app's real moat is therefore its **feedback loop**: approve/skip prompts, per-sender overrides and "Fix this rule" actions, which together produce calibrated, personal training labels as a side effect of use. Build that loop first and every classifier route improves over time. Without it, even GLiNER-class decision models stall near 60%.

Two uncertainties deserve early experiments rather than more reading. The first is whether Qwen3 8B logprobs, temperature-scaled on a few hundred labels, are calibrated well enough to auto-file mail without review. The second is whether POLITEMall accepts the cookie-to-Bearer token mint. Both can be answered within a week with the student's own data, and the answers decide how much of the pipeline can run unattended.
